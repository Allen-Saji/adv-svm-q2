The Banking Jitter Lab, Explained From Zero

No prior knowledge assumed. We start with "what is a block," build up every word in the assignment one at a time, and only at the very end show what the lab actually measures. If a term feels heavy, there is an analogy right next to it.

Level: beginner Assumes: nothing Read time: ~18 min Goal: understand the lab
The lab in one sentence

Solana gives the computer that builds a block a strict time budget. This lab builds a stopwatch that times the block-building code against that budget, and then figures out why the time sometimes wobbles.

00What are we even building?

The 30-second version, before any jargon.

You are building a small measurement program (the word for this is a harness). It does three things:

  • Run the real Solana block-building code in a controlled way, over and over.
  • Time it with a stopwatch on each run.
  • Compare that time against the deadline Solana's consensus rules allow, and explain any spikes.

That's it. Everything else in this note is just defining the words: "block-building code," "deadline," and "why it spikes." Let's earn each one.

Hold this analogy the whole way

Think of a restaurant kitchen during a lunch rush. Every 400 milliseconds the kitchen must plate one finished dish (a block) and pass it down the line. The "banking stage" is the cook. "Jitter" is when one dish suddenly takes way longer than the others. Your lab is the kitchen timer that catches the slow plate and asks: was the cook waiting for the oven, waiting for a free counter, or waiting for ingredients to be delivered?

01Prerequisite: blocks, slots, and the leader

Who builds a block, and how often.

A blockchain is a list of blocks. Each block is a batch of transactions that everyone agrees happened. On Solana, a new block is produced roughly every 400 milliseconds. That fixed 400 ms window is called a slot.

At any moment, one specific validator (one computer in the network) is chosen to build the block for the current slot. That chosen computer is the leader. The leadership rotates: you are leader for a few slots, then someone else is, on a published schedule.

The key constraint

The leader has only one slot (~400 ms) to collect transactions, run them, and ship the finished block. Miss that window and the block is late or skipped. This deadline is the entire reason your lab exists.

Time is chopped into fixed 400 ms slots. In your slot, you are the leader and must produce one block before the window closes.

02Prerequisite: the banking stage

The part of the leader that actually builds the block.

Inside the leader, a transaction passes through an assembly line called the TPU (Transaction Processing Unit). Each station does one job:

The leader's assembly line (TPU). The banking stage is the star: it is where transactions are actually executed and packed into the block. (PoH is drawn as its own box for clarity, but the stamping actually happens inside the banking stage: it is the record phase you'll meet next.)

The banking stage is the cook in our kitchen analogy. It takes verified transactions and does the real work: run each transaction's code, update account balances, and hand the result to be recorded in the block. Because this is where the heavy lifting happens, this is where time gets spent, and therefore where time can be wasted. That is why the lab points its stopwatch here and nowhere else.

In the code

This lives in Agave (the main Solana validator software) at core/src/banking_stage.rs. Agave is just the name of the production codebase your harness compiles against.

03Prerequisite: the consumer and its four phases

Breaking "build the block" into four smaller timed steps.

Inside the banking stage, the component that turns transactions into finished, recorded block-entries is called the consumer. When it processes a batch of transactions, it goes through four phases in order. The lab times each one separately, because knowing which phase is slow tells you a lot.

PhasePlain meaningKitchen analogy
load_executeLoad the accounts the transaction touches, then run the transaction's program.Fetch ingredients and actually cook.
freeze_lockBriefly take the bank's freeze lock so the block's state can't be sealed mid-write. This is not the per-account write lock, and it sits near zero unless another thread is freezing the bank.Glance up to confirm the kitchen isn't being closed while you plate.
recordWrite the result into the Proof-of-History stream (the official ordering).Write the dish onto the official order ticket.
commitMake the new balances permanent in the bank's state.Serve the dish; it's now real.
Why split it up

"The block took 30 ms" is not actionable. "The block took 30 ms and 28 of them were in load_execute" tells you exactly where to look. The four-phase breakdown is the difference between a vague complaint and a diagnosis.

A trap to avoid with freeze_lock

It is tempting to think freeze_lock is what serializes transactions that touch the same account. It is not. When two transactions fight over the same account, that contention shows up as a drop in how many transactions commit (the losers are serialized out and retried), not as freeze_lock time. freeze_lock guards a different thing (sealing the bank) and stays near zero in normal single-batch runs. The harness has a dedicated third test that demonstrates exactly this, so the two locks never get confused.

04Prerequisite: Votor and the deadline

Where the time budget actually comes from.

A block is not "done" when the leader finishes building it. The rest of the network has to vote to agree on it. That voting-and-agreement process is consensus. Solana's next-generation consensus design is called Alpenglow, and the specific part that runs the voting is named Votor.

Votor's design says: from the moment a block starts being shared, agreement must finish within a certain time, or the network moves on. That allowed time is the budget. The whitepaper expresses it in symbols, but here is the plain version your harness uses:

SymbolPlain meaningValue used
ΔblockThe normal block time. How long the leader gets to produce the block.400 ms
δOne network hop. Time for a message to cross from one validator to another.~50 ms (assumed)
3·δA safety margin for consensus: three network hops' worth of slack.~150 ms
TimeoutThe full give-up deadline = 3·δ + Δblock (safety margin + block time).~550 ms
The subtle but important point

The 550 ms Timeout is a liveness ceiling: it is the "if we pass this, give up and move on" line, not the target. The number the block-builder is actually judged against is Δblock = 400 ms. Your banking stage must finish its work comfortably inside 400 ms. The lab treats 400 ms as the deadline and checks every run against it.

A common trap

Votor does not run in today's Solana yet (today's network uses an older consensus called TowerBFT). So you cannot "measure Votor." You instead borrow Votor's budget number and use it as a deadline to grade the banking stage. The lab measures real banking code against a future consensus's clock.

05Prerequisite: what "jitter" means

The actual thing being studied.

Run the same code ten times and you will not get the same time ten times. Maybe nine runs take 2 ms and one run takes 32 ms. That inconsistency is jitter. Formally: jitter = slowest run − fastest run.

Why jitter, not average, is the enemy

If your commute averages 20 minutes but once a week takes 90, the average looks fine while you keep missing meetings. Blockchains live and die by the worst case, not the average, because one late block hurts everyone. So we hunt the spike, not the mean.

When a spike happens, it is caused by one of three things. The whole skill of this lab is telling them apart:

The three jitter buckets. A spike is always one of these. The reference harness guessed which; the goal of our version is to measure which.
The learning goal, stated plainly

The assignment's real skill is diagnosis: given a spike, prove whether it was CPU scheduling, an I/O stall, or an allocator pause, using evidence instead of a hunch.

06Prerequisite: tokio vs OS threads (and the blind spot)

Why the obvious debugging tool quietly fails here.

Programs do many things at once using threads (independent lines of work). There are two flavors here, and the difference is the whole trick:

KindWhat it isCan a tokio debugger see it?
tokio taskA lightweight job managed by a Rust library called tokio. There is a popular tool, tokio-console, that shows these live.Yes
OS threadA raw thread managed directly by the operating system, wired together with plain channels.No

Here is the catch. The banking stage looks like a tokio program on the surface, but the real work, the part that actually spends time, runs on raw OS threads. So if you reach for tokio-console (the natural first move), it shows you the manager sitting idle and tells you nothing about the busy workers. The tool is blind to exactly the place that matters.

Why this is a feature of the lab, not a bug

Discovering that tokio-console is blind here is itself a core lesson. A big part of the deliverable is demonstrating the blind spot on purpose, then reaching for the right tools (operating-system level timers and counters) to see the worker threads.

07Putting it together: what the harness does

Now every word in the assignment is defined. Here is the whole thing.

The lab is built as two stopwatches plus a contention probe. The two stopwatches are two useful zoom levels; the third test settles a common confusion:

  • End-to-end stopwatch. Send one transaction into the real banking stage and time how long until it comes out as a finished, committed block-entry. This answers: "did the whole thing fit inside the 400 ms budget?"
  • Per-phase stopwatch. Call the consumer directly and read the built-in timers for the four phases (load_execute, freeze_lock, record, commit). This answers: "when a run is slow, which phase ate the time?"
  • Account-lock contention probe. Submit a batch of transactions that all touch the same account and watch how many actually commit. This answers: "what does real contention look like?" and proves it shows up as a committed-count drop, not as freeze_lock time.
Two zoom levels. Stopwatch 1 answers "did we fit the budget?" Stopwatch 2 answers "where did the time go?"

You run both many times, throw away nothing, and print a small report: minimum, average, maximum, and the jitter (max minus min) for each measurement, plus a PASS/OVER verdict against the 400 ms budget.

08What the numbers actually say

The headline result, in one breath.

Across runs, almost everything is fast: steady-state banking is roughly 200 microseconds end to end. But the very first run is slow: about 30 milliseconds, on the order of 100x slower. After that, everything settles down. This first-run spike is a classic cold start: the very first time through, the program pays one-time costs that later runs no longer pay.

The interesting part is which one-time cost. The obvious guess is "the code is just doing more work the first time": running transactions, writing memory. The harness measures instead of guessing, and the obvious guess turns out to be wrong:

  • Not the CPU. During the ~30 ms cold window, all the banking worker threads combined use under 1 ms of CPU. They are barely working.
  • Not the allocator. A timing allocator shows about the same microseconds in malloc/free on the cold run as on a warm one.
  • Not scheduling, not disk. Run-queue wait is ~0 and there are zero major page faults.
  • What it actually is. The worker threads are asleep, parked in a kernel wait (futex_wait_queue), and the spike is the time to wake them up and deliver the first message, plus a burst of first-touch page faults (~2200 vs ~170 steady) as fresh memory is paged in for the very first time.
The honest takeaway

Steady-state banking is comfortably inside the 400 ms budget, roughly 1000x of headroom. The jitter is almost entirely the cold-start spike, and the spike is wakeup and first-touch paging, not computation. That distinction is the entire payoff of measuring instead of guessing: the slow first run is the operating system waking sleeping threads and paging in memory, not the banking code doing heavy work.

The difference between repeating the exercise and owning it.
